# 2. PEB, Sandwich Panels and the Data-Centre Thesis

## 2.1 What a PEB is and how it works

A **pre-engineered building (PEB)** is a steel building whose components are **designed and fabricated in a factory and then bolted together at the site** — engineered to the customer's exact dimensions, wind/seismic loads, machinery and cranes.

**Process:** customer brief → **structural design** (STAAD/Tekla, at Noida/Hyderabad/Vizag centres, 97 engineers) → **detailing** into fabrication drawings → **fabrication** (cutting, welding built-up steel sections) → surface treatment → **logistics** → **site erection**. EPACK's pitch: *"we design, fabricate, transport, and erect — under one roof, one contract, one accountability."*

A typical project takes **6–8 months** (some ~4.5 months); the steel erection itself can be days — the Mambattu 1.5 lakh sq ft build took **150 hours**. Note the 150-hour figure is **erection only**, not design-to-handover.

## 2.2 PEB vs traditional RCC

| Dimension | PEB | RCC / conventional |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | **40–60% faster** (structural work ~2–3 months vs 8–12) | Slower, weather/site-dependent |
| Weight / steel | Frames **30–40% lighter**; ~30% less steel | Heavier; RCC's steel is reinforcement |
| Cost | ~**20–35% cheaper for large single-storey industrial** | Comparable or cheaper for multi-storey, heavy point-load, high fire-rating |
| Labour / waste | Much lower site labour, less waste, cleaner | Labour-intensive, more waste |
| Flexibility | Easy to expand/reconfigure/move | Hard to modify |
| Sustainability | Recyclable steel; insulated envelope cuts HVAC load (company cites 52% lower embodied carbon) | Higher embodied carbon |

The real advantage is **speed + flexibility + lower site labour** for large industrial sheds. The benefit isn't only cost — **a factory or data centre delivered 4–6 months sooner starts earning revenue sooner**, which often exceeds the per-sq-ft cost delta. But PEB **puts EPACK's input cost on steel (~65–70% of material cost)**, which is why fixed-price contracts and steel spikes are the core margin risk.

## 2.3 Sandwich panels

A **sandwich panel** = metal skin + insulating core + metal skin. Cores: PUF/PUR (thermal), **PIR** (better fire performance), **Rockwool/Glasswool** (fire-resistant, for data centres/clean rooms). Used in walls/roofs of cold storage, food/pharma clean rooms, warehouses, data centres and facades.

- Capacity grew **0.51m → 1.31m sqm**; a Ghiloth line adds ~0.8m sqm (→2.11m).
- **Utilisation is poor:** ~25% at Mambattu in FY26, ~44% in Q1 FY27. The AR is internally inconsistent (44.1% in the KPI table vs 39.6% in the risk section, pp. 61/68).
- Why low: management admits the **sales/go-to-market engine wasn't built**. Fix: new sales leadership, dedicated cold-chain/food-processing teams, data-centre focus.
- Economics: one 8 lakh sqm line at ₹1,700–2,000/sqm = **₹125–140 cr pure panel revenue**, or ~**₹250 cr including attached structural work**. FY26 panel production was only ~5.18 lakh sqm ≈ ₹65 cr (not separately reported).
- Margins "a little better" than plain structural; line is **highly automated with low operating cost**, so filling it is genuine operating leverage. Strategic value: EPACK captures the envelope margin in-house instead of outsourcing, and offers single-point coordination, which helps win integrated projects.

**Why this matters:** panels are the single biggest swing factor in margins. Filling them to 70%+ is high-margin incremental revenue; leaving them at 44% while adding more capacity is capital misallocation.

## 2.4 The data-centre opportunity — real vs narrative

**Why the thesis is logical:**
- India DC capacity going from ~900–950 MW (FY24) to ~2,000–2,300 MW (FY27), ~30% CAGR, with credible forecasts of 4–8 GW by 2030 and a **~$30bn construction opportunity** (KPMG, SBI, JLL). Semicon 2.0 and Budget 2026-27 DC tax incentives add tailwinds.
- **Speed matters** (DCs earn nothing until live), and **the thermal envelope matters** (cooling is a large share of DC operating cost) — exactly where PEB + fire-rated PIR/rockwool panels play.

**But the substance today is narrow:**
- EPACK currently supplies **mostly sandwich panels for walls/facades** (incl. an order for **Adani's upcoming DC**), plus some structural. It has done 4–5 DC panel projects.
- **Data centres are only ~4–5% of the order book**, and only **₹15 cr / 0.9% of FY26 order bookings**.
- It wants the **full turnkey package** — structural frame, **hot/cold aisle containment, pipe spooling, P&M modules, MEP integration** — worth ~₹60–110 cr per building. It has **lost two complete DC structure bids** on design optimisation and pricing.
- Capability (pipe spooling equipment, M&E integration) is still being built; P&M modules expected within 3–4 months of the Aug call.
- The **EPACK Data Center Solutions** subsidiary (incorporated 31 Aug 2026, ₹10 lakh paid-up, ₹75 cr in-principle) has **no concrete plan, site, funding or ROCE target yet** — management conceded this on the Aug call.
- Competition: **L&T/NCC** (EPC primes), **JSW Severfield** (already served CtrlS/AdaniConneX/Colt), JSPL and other PEB players. The opportunity is where a prime EPC outsources the structural package; weaker where a steel major keeps it in-house.

**Conclusion:** the DC narrative is a credible **option**, not an earnings driver. The real near-term play is **premium fire-rated panels**. Treat the ₹75 cr subsidiary and "full DC turnkey" as speculative until a large order is actually won. Also note the addressable slice is small: civil+structural is only **~12–20% of a DC's ₹40–95 cr/MW capex**.

# 3. Cash-Flow Deep-Dive and Growth Feasibility

## 3.1 First, a correction on the mechanics

Receivables did **not** shrink. **Trade receivables grew 50% (₹205.3 cr → ₹308.8 cr)**, which is a **cash outflow**. What increased on the customer side was **contract liabilities / advances from customers** (₹66.6 cr → ₹111.4 cr), which is a **cash inflow**. In the cash-flow statement these appear in two different lines:

- "Increase in trade receivables" → **−₹103.4 cr** (cash used)
- "Increase in other current liabilities" → **+₹52.2 cr**, of which **contract liabilities +₹44.7 cr** (cash received in advance)

So the customer-related tailwind came from **advances, not from receivables shrinking**.

## 3.2 The FY26 cash flow, stripped back (consolidated, ₹ lakh)

|---|---:|---:|

| + D&A, finance cost, ESOP, etc., − interest income | +3,957 | +3,807 |

| Trade receivables | **(10,342)** | (7,880) |
| Inventories | **(11,428)** | (1,360) |

## 3.3 What really happened: payables and advances funded the growth

The headline "CFO = ₹136 cr ≈ 85% of EBITDA" looks healthy, but **look at what produced it**:

- Receivables + inventories consumed **₹217.7 cr** of cash.
- That was more than offset by **₹174.5 cr of higher trade payables** and **₹52.2 cr of higher other current liabilities (incl. ₹44.7 cr customer advances)**.

**Strip out the payable stretch and the advance inflow, and FY26 operating cash flow would have been materially negative.** The company funded its working-capital build by (a) paying suppliers later and (b) collecting customer advances earlier.

**Trade payables detail (AR pp. 265–266, 289):**
- Payables rose **₹223.3 cr → ₹397.8 cr** (+78%, vs revenue +34.5%).
- **₹131.3 cr is a "supplier finance" / reverse-factoring arrangement** via the M1xchange platform, with payment terms **up to 120 days** (normal trade credit is 30–60 days).
- Payable days jumped **103 → 143**; the ₹131 cr is economically **closer to borrowing** than trade credit, but sits in trade payables and does **not** appear as debt.

**Why the payables increase is a quality concern:**
1. It flatters working capital and CFO — the "32-day cycle" relies on stretching vendors.
2. Supplier finance can be withdrawn if banks/platforms tighten, instantly reversing the cash benefit.
3. Suppliers may price in the delay, raising input costs.
4. It hides leverage: strip it out and the "net cash" position and debt ratios look less pristine.

**Why rising customer advances (contract liabilities) is also a concern:**
1. Under Ind AS 115 percentage-of-completion, revenue is recognised as **costs are incurred**; advances are a **liability until the work is done**. They boost cash before they boost revenue.
2. It's an obligation to deliver — if execution slips (as in Q3 FY26 with customer civil delays), advances unwind and cash flow weakens.
3. It can mask weak collection on completed work.

**And receivables growth is genuinely worrying:**
- Receivables +50% vs revenue +34.5% → **debtor days 66 → 74**.
- Ageing (AR p. 255): only **₹14.3 cr not yet due**; **₹199.3 cr 0–6 months**; **₹61.3 cr 6–12 months**; **₹37.2 cr over 12 months** (₹22.7 cr 1–2 yrs, ₹10.1 cr 2–3 yrs, ₹0.5 cr >3 yrs). So **~₹98.5 cr is past due over 6 months**.
- The **ECL provision is only ₹3.46 cr** — thin against ₹37 cr of >12-month receivables and ₹92.6 cr of FY26 PAT.
- Management calls it recoverable; the evidence isn't yet there.

Meanwhile **inventory rose 75%** (raw materials +131%, WIP +42%) — cash tied up before billing.

## 3.4 Is the growth rate feasible?

**Operationally, yes; financially, it is not self-funding — and that's the key finding.**

Operational support:
- Order book ₹1,376 cr (+40%), FY27 order-inflow target ₹2,000 cr+, Q1 inflow ₹578 cr (+142%), PEB utilisation 75%+, capacity going to 220k MTPA. Revenue of ₹1,900–1,950 cr (+25–28%) is achievable on these inputs.

But the financial engine has three dependencies:
1. **IPO cash is doing the heavy lifting.** FY26 financing inflow was ₹284 cr from shares; investing outflow was ₹303 cr (capex + treasury). FY26 free cash flow was roughly **₹0 cr** (negative FY22–25). FY27 capex is guided ~₹150 cr (₹280 cr planned), so FCF will likely stay negative unless CFO improves sharply. Without the IPO, the model doesn't self-fund.
2. **Supplier finance + payables** (₹131 cr) are masking true working-capital intensity.
3. **Customer advances** are providing free float.

**The math of the constraint:** at ~9% incremental working capital on revenue, growing revenue by ~₹400 cr needs ~₹36 cr of incremental WC — manageable. But if debtor/inventory days keep rising (74 and 96 now), the cash requirement is higher, and it must come from either more payable stretch or fresh capital.

**Other fading tailwinds:** interest income of **₹14.9 cr** (inside ₹17.2 cr other income) is ~12% of PBT and will shrink as IPO cash is spent on capex — so PAT growth can lag operating growth even if execution is fine.

**What to watch to judge feasibility:**
- CFO/EBITDA conversion staying ≥80% **without** further payable expansion — if cash flow rises only because payables balloon, quality is deteriorating.
- Receivables past-due >6 months and ECL provisioning.
- Whether the 32-day working-capital cycle holds once supplier finance and advances normalise (management itself guides 35–38 days).
- FY27 capex funded without new debt/equity (management says no incremental debt planned — that claim becomes the test).
- Any return of FCF-positive cash generation as capacity matures.

**Bottom line:** growth is **physically feasible but financially fragile**. EPACK is converting IPO money, supplier credit and customer advances into a larger working-capital base and new capacity. If margins recover to 10.5%+ and collections hold, the model self-sustains over time. If steel margins stay compressed (Q1 FY27 9.4%) or receivables slip, the company will consume its cash buffer and may need to raise again — the opposite of the "net cash, deleveraged" story the market is currently paying for.

# 4. PEB vs RCC: Market Share, Building Types and the Developed-Market Precedent

## 4.1 Short answer

PEB is **not replacing conventional construction wholesale** — it is winning decisively in **industrial, warehousing and low-rise logistics** buildings, and barely touching **residential and multi-storey commercial offices**. The transition is real but slow: it took the US and UK about **35–40 years** to reach today's ~40–65% share of low-rise non-residential. India is at the beginning of that curve (~3–5% of construction activity, ~13–15% of industrial construction), which is exactly why it's an attractive long runway — but also why "PEB is the future" should be qualified.

## 4.2 India: current penetration and trajectory

| Metric | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| PEB as % of **overall** construction activity | **~3–5%** | CRISIL, via DAM Capital/ICICI/DRHP |
| PEB as % of **industrial** construction | **~13–15%** | CRISIL/DAM Capital, FY25 |
| PEB as % of **infrastructure** | ~5–7% | ICICI Direct |
| PEB as % of **buildings** (residential + commercial) | **~0–1%** | ICICI/DRHP |
| Organised share of PEB industry | **42–47%** (FY25), up from ~32% FY18 | Ambit/ICICI |
| India PEB market size | **₹13,000 cr (FY19) → ₹21,000 cr (FY25) → ₹33,000–34,500 cr (FY30E)** | CRISIL |
| PEB CAGR | **~8.3% (FY19–25) → ~10% (FY25–30E)** | CRISIL |
| India overall construction CAGR | **~6–7%** | Mordor/GlobalData |

Two important nuances:

- **Denominator caution.** "3–5% of construction" is *not* a value share. India's construction market is ~₹22.8 lakh crore; PEB revenue of ₹21,000 cr is only **~0.9% of construction value**. The 3–5% figure refers to *share of structural-system/project activity*. A metal building is only ~20% of total project cost, which reconciles the two.
- **Conflicting vendor claims.** A paid report claims PEB is already **35–40% of new industrial shed/warehouse construction** — that conflicts sharply with CRISIL's ~13–15% of industrial construction. Treat CRISIL as the anchor and the vendor number as an inflated/differently-defined outlier.

**Direction of travel:** PEB grows ~10% vs overall construction ~6–7%, so penetration is rising (to ~5–7% by FY30) — but the bigger return driver is **organised consolidation** (42–47% → 47–52%) and the **industrial capex cycle**, not a step-change in overall penetration. Steel-intensity runway: India uses **97.7 kg of steel per capita** vs the world average of **215 kg**, China **646 kg**, Japan **444 kg**, US **279 kg** (worldsteel 2024).

## 4.3 Where PEB works — and where it doesn't

**PEB is the default choice for (industrial + infrastructure ≈ 90% of demand):**

| Building type | PEB suitability |
|---|---|
| Warehouses / 3PL logistics | **Preferred / dominant** (2–3 months vs 8–12 for RCC) |
| Factories, industrial sheds, PLI plants | **Preferred** |
| Data centres, semiconductor fabs | Rapidly growing; frame + fire-rated panels |
| Cold storage / food processing / clean rooms | **Preferred** (sandwich panels) |
| Airports, metro depots, railway yards, toll plazas, hangars | Common (long-span) |
| Sports stadia, exhibition halls, defence | Common |
| Retail showrooms, multi-level car parks | Common |
| Institutional (schools, hospitals) | Used, especially low-rise |

**PEB is NOT generally used for:**

| Building type | Why not |
|---|---|
| **Multi-storey residential** | Steel tapered frames optimised for bending, not cumulative axial loads; floor slabs, acoustics, fire-rating and vibration favour RCC |
| **High-rise commercial offices** | Same reasons; concrete/composite dominates |
| Heavy vibrating machinery / strict fire-rated structures | RCC often preferred |
| Dense, compact column grids | Steel can cost **~38% more** on direct structural cost in identical-geometry cases |

**Residential:** the credible steel route is **LGSF (light-gauge steel framing)**, but only for **1–3 storey** buildings. India's LGSF market is ~USD 1.9 bn growing ~6.3% — slower than PEB. Policy is supportive (NBC 2016 Part 6; CPWD LGSF rates; PMAY Light House Projects; 16 lakh+ houses using new technologies), but residential steel penetration is realistically a **10–20 year story**.

**Hybrids are common and growing:** PEB roof/structure on an RCC podium, or steel frames with composite metal-deck floors.

## 4.4 Developed-market precedent — yes, in industrial, not residential

### United States — Metal Building Systems (MBMA data)

| Year | MBS share of low-rise non-residential |
|---|---|
| 1956 | 20% |
| 1964 | 24% |
| 1970 | 30% |
| 1981 | 56% |
| 1989 | 57% |
| 1995 | 65% (1–2 storey ≤150,000 sq ft) |
| Today | ~40–50% (definitional drift) |

**Timeline: ~35–40 years (1956 → mid-1990s) to go from 20% to ~60–65%**, then plateau. MBMA members still represent only ~$2.4 bn in annual shipments and ~35–50% of low-rise non-residential. Growth was driven by first-cost advantage, cost predictability, speed and engineering efficiency — not a mandate.

### United Kingdom

- **98% of single-storey shed buildings** are steel; **~65% of multi-storey non-domestic** floor area; **94.1% of industrial buildings** steel (BCSA, 2024/25).
- Took roughly **1980s → today (~40 years)**, driven by offsite efficiency, the 2017 government P.DfMA presumption, and labour/productivity pressures.
- But **UK housing remains largely masonry/concrete** — the transition did not reach residential.

### China

- Prefab went from **<10% (2015) → 20.5% (2020) → 35.6% (2025)** of new construction area — a state-driven ~10-year transition.
- **But it's precast concrete (~21% of new area), not steel (~11.7%)**, that drove the residential/multi-storey shift.
- In **industrial buildings, steel is ~78.4%** — proving steel dominates factories/warehouses, not housing.

### Japan, Singapore, Nordics

- **Japan:** prefab housing ~13% and *declining* — a caution that residential prefab doesn't inevitably gain share.
- **Singapore:** DfMA adoption ~68% of gross floor area (2024), state-mandated via land-sale conditions — the strongest policy-driven example, again largely precast concrete.
- **Nordics (Sweden ~45%, Finland ~half of housing):** the only developed markets near prefab-dominant housing — but **timber-based and cold-climate driven**, not steel PEB.

### The pattern

| Market | Transition reached | Took | Driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| US | Low-rise non-residential ~40–50% | ~35–40 yrs | Cost, speed, MBMA ecosystem |
| UK | Sheds 98%, multi-storey non-domestic ~65% | ~40 yrs | Offsite policy + labour |
| China | Industrial steel 78%; overall prefab 36% | ~10 yrs | State mandate |
| Singapore | DfMA 68% of GFA | Mandated | Government land sales |
| **Residential (US/Germany/Australia)** | **~3–15%** | — | Weak/limited |

**No advanced economy has made steel/prefab the dominant system for mainstream multi-storey housing.** The transition is an **industrial, logistics and low-rise commercial** phenomenon.

## 4.5 So is PEB "the future"? — a qualified yes

**Yes, for:** factories, warehouses, logistics, cold storage, data centres, semiconductor fabs, airports, metro/rail, defence, stadia and low-rise industrial/commercial. This is where PEB is structurally displacing RCC, and India is early (13–15% industrial penetration vs the US/UK's ~60–98%). Drivers are durable: labour scarcity, speed (40–50% faster), cost (5–35%), quality, carbon, and policy (PLI, Gati Shakti, Semicon Mission, National Logistics Policy).

**No, not yet, for:** mainstream residential and multi-storey commercial offices. Technical limits (floor slabs, fire, acoustic, vibration, point loads), fragmented permitting and the incumbent RCC contractor ecosystem keep building-sector penetration at ~0–1%, forecast to only ~1% by FY29. Residential steel is a long-dated option (LGSF, 1–3 storeys), not a near-term story.

**What this means for EPACK specifically:** it validates the **industrial + logistics + data-centre + renewable** core of the investment case and explains why management keeps pointing there. It also **caps the "going into commercial/residential" upside** — a genuine runway but a slow one, and not the reason to own the stock. The more realistic near-to-medium-term re-rating drivers remain (a) share gain within industrial capex, (b) organised consolidation, (c) panel utilisation, and (d) margin normalisation — not a broad-based shift away from RCC across the whole construction market.
