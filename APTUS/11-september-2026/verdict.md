# Aptus Value Housing Finance India Ltd (APTUS)

**Analysis date:** 11 September 2026  
**Reference:** Screener consolidated page, latest available Q1 FY27 operating disclosures, FY26 annual report, investor presentations and earnings-call transcripts.

## Verdict

**Status: high-quality franchise, but currently a monitored/ selective buy rather than a clean compounder.**

Aptus has an unusually profitable and well-capitalised affordable-finance model: FY26 consolidated AUM was ₹13,107 crore, PAT was ₹943 crore, ROA was 7.9%, ROE was 20.1%, average LTV was around 40%, and standalone CRAR was approximately 71%. Its in-house sourcing, underwriting and collections platform, long operating history and diversified institutional funding are genuine strengths.

The important qualification is that this is not a single homogeneous housing lender. The HFC business is comparatively resilient; the 100%-owned NBFC subsidiary, which is 29% of AUM and focuses on higher-yielding small-business loans against residential property, is driving the recent deterioration. In Q1 FY27, consolidated GNPA rose from 1.52% to 1.70%, collection efficiency fell to 98.52%, NBFC collection efficiency was approximately 97.5% versus 99.5% for HFC, and NBFC credit cost was approximately 1.4% versus 0.2% for HFC. Management attributes this to a temporary June collection issue and said Stage 2 improved in July, but that explanation requires confirmation in subsequent quarters.

At the 11 September Screener snapshot, APTUS traded at ₹249.11, with market capitalisation of approximately ₹12,466 crore and P/E of 12.66x. Against FY26 net worth of approximately ₹5,060 crore, the implied price/book is roughly 2.5x. This is not an extreme valuation for a lender generating about 20% ROE, but it already assumes that growth remains near 20% and the NBFC asset-quality problem does not become structural. The stock can be attractive for a long-term investor who accepts credit-cycle risk, but the evidence does not justify underwriting a smooth 22%-24% AUM and 20%+ ROE trajectory without a monitoring discount.

## Business Model and Franchise

Aptus is an NHB-registered, non-deposit-taking HFC focused on self-employed and informal-income borrowers in Tier-2 to Tier-4 locations. The HFC lends for home construction, purchase, renovation and extension, as well as quasi-home and top-up products. Its wholly owned subsidiary, Aptus Finance India, is an RBI-registered NBFC focused on secured small-business loans against residential property.

The operating model is largely internal: branch sourcing, field verification, credit underwriting, legal and technical appraisal, and collections are not materially dependent on DSAs or franchise branches. The company uses bureau data, account-aggregator data, digital agreements, land records and field-level cash-flow assessment. The low average LTV, generally around 35%-40%, is a meaningful loss-severity mitigant, although it does not remove repayment, recovery-timing or property-liquidity risk.

The network had 339 branches at March 2026 and 372 at June 2026. The group operates mainly in Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Telangana and Karnataka, with Maharashtra and Odisha still early-stage. At FY26, HFC AUM was ₹9,306 crore and NBFC AUM was ₹3,801 crore. Home loans were approximately 67%-68% of HFC AUM, with quasi-home loans making up most of the balance; the NBFC portfolio was small-business/non-home lending.

### What could be durable

- Local underwriting knowledge in informal-income borrower segments.
- In-house collections and a branch-led model that avoids excessive channel dependence.
- Low LTV and secured residential collateral.
- High ROA and strong capitalisation, leaving room to increase leverage.
- Branch-vintage economics that show mature branches producing far more AUM than new branches.
- Digital processes that are operationally relevant: approximately 94% of collections and over 92% of agreements were digital in recent disclosures.

### What is not yet proven

- Whether branch productivity in established Southern markets can be replicated in Maharashtra and Odisha.
- Whether the higher-yielding NBFC book can grow without a disproportionate rise in credit costs.
- Whether the company can sustain operating expenses near 2.7%-2.8% of AUM while adding 60-70 branches annually and hiring more collections staff.
- Whether the post-₹7 lakh minimum-ticket policy improves risk-adjusted returns without permanently reducing the addressable customer pool.

## Financial Quality

### Growth and profitability

| Consolidated metric | FY25 | FY26 | Change / observation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| AUM | ₹10,865 cr | ₹13,107 cr | 21% growth |
| Total income | ₹1,798 cr | ₹2,245 cr | 25% growth |
| PAT | ₹751 cr | ₹943 cr | 26% growth |
| Net worth | ₹4,317 cr | ₹5,060 cr | 17% growth |
| ROA | 7.7% | 7.9% | Very strong for the sector |
| ROE | 18.8% | 20.1% | Improved despite moderate leverage |
| Cost of borrowings | 8.7% | 8.3% | Funding-led spread support |
| GNPA | 1.19% | 1.52% | Material deterioration |
| NNPA | 0.89% | 1.15% | Material deterioration |

Screener shows revenue increasing from ₹1,093 crore in FY23 to ₹2,192 crore in FY26 and TTM PAT of ₹985 crore. Five-year sales and profit growth are approximately 28%-29%, with five-year ROE around 18%. Q1 FY27 continued to show strong headline growth: AUM rose 21% year on year to ₹13,648 crore, disbursements rose 36% to ₹1,053 crore, and PAT rose 19% to approximately ₹261 crore.

The quality of the headline PAT number needs some normalisation. The FY26 annual report records a ₹172.44 crore consolidated gain on derecognition of financial instruments, versus ₹16.31 crore in FY25. This gain is approximately 14% of FY26 PBT. It may be economically valid transaction income, but it is not a recurring lending spread and should not be capitalised as if it will repeat every year.

### Margins and funding

Portfolio yield was approximately 17.2% in FY26 and 17.1% in Q1 FY27. Cost of borrowings declined to approximately 8.3% in FY26 and 8.0%-8.1% in Q1 FY27, producing a spread near 9.0%. The improvement was primarily liability-led; loan yields were stable to slightly lower as the company reduced rates on selected ticket sizes.

This margin structure is favourable in a falling or stable-rate environment, but the asset-liability mismatch matters: approximately 82% of loans are fixed-rate while a substantial part of borrowings is floating-rate. Management estimated that a repo-rate increase would have a modest immediate impact, but competition and funding spreads could still compress NIM over time.

Funding is diversified across banks, NCDs, NHB refinance and securitisation/direct assignment, with more than 25 lender relationships and no commercial paper. The average borrowing tenor is approximately 80 months. FY26 LCR was 212%, and management reported roughly ₹1,933 crore of liquidity at June 2026, including unavailed bank sanctions. These are strong safeguards, but the business structurally funds loan growth through incremental borrowing and portfolio monetisation.

### Cash flow and leverage

Consolidated operating cash flow was negative ₹489 crore in FY26 despite ₹943 crore PAT; free cash flow was negative ₹496 crore. This is expected for a lender expanding its loan book, because loan disbursements are classified within operating cash flows. It is not automatically a red flag, but it means growth depends on continued lender access, securitisation capacity and liquidity management rather than retained operating cash.

Capitalisation is a major strength. Standalone CRAR was approximately 71% at March 2026 and around 70% at June 2026. Management believes this can support 22%-24% AUM growth and intends to increase leverage toward approximately 3x. The upside is higher ROE without immediate equity dilution; the risk is that leverage amplifies the effect of a future credit-cost surprise. The unusually high CRAR also indicates that the current 20% ROE is being achieved with considerable capital conservatism.

## Asset Quality: The Critical Issue

The recent data are mixed rather than disastrous, but the direction is adverse.

| Metric | FY25 | FY26 | Q1 FY27 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| GNPA | 1.19% | 1.52% | 1.70% |
| NNPA | 0.89% | 1.15% | 1.29% |
| 30+ DPD | Not comparable | 6.21% at Q4 | 6.87% |
| Collection efficiency | Not comparable | 100.50% at Q4 | 98.52% |
| NBFC credit cost | Not separately disclosed | Higher than HFC | Approximately 1.4% |
| HFC credit cost | Not separately disclosed | Lower than NBFC | Approximately 0.2% |

Management says the Q1 deterioration was caused by missed NBFC commitments at the end of June, that June dues were paid in July, that July commitments were honoured, and that Stage 2 assets fell by 15-20 bps in July. This is plausible, particularly given seasonal collection patterns, but it remains a management claim until Q2 data show the promised reversal.

There are three reasons not to dismiss the issue as purely seasonal:

1. Stage 2 and Stage 3 assets both increased in FY26, not just the headline NPA ratio.
2. The NBFC business has higher yields, higher cash-flow dependence and weaker Q1 collections than HFC.
3. The company changed its technical write-off policy from 100% write-off after two years to 100% after 500 days. This increased reported credit cost and makes historical comparisons less like-for-like.

The annual report says the FY26 increase in GNPA was primarily due to the NBFC SBL portfolio and identifies Karnataka as a weaker-collection geography. It also reports FY26 provision coverage of approximately 25%. Low LTV helps potential recoveries, but a secured loan can still generate losses through weak documentation, delayed enforcement, local property illiquidity, foreclosure costs and borrower cash-flow stress.

The company has committed to providing separate HFC and NBFC data from September 2026. This is important. Consolidated GNPA currently masks a meaningful difference in risk and economics between the two entities.

## Management and Governance

Management has demonstrated a willingness to sacrifice some growth: it stopped sourcing proposals below ₹7 lakh to improve customer quality and reduce collection intensity, even though management said the existing low-ticket book did not show materially worse NPAs. FY26 growth moderated to 21%, below the earlier 25%+ ambition, and guidance was reset to 22%-24% for FY27.

That is a positive signal if underwriting discipline is genuine. However, management explanations should be tested against outcomes. The Q1 FY27 call repeatedly called the NBFC collection issue temporary despite objective deterioration. There are also unresolved communication issues:

- FY27 branch guidance is approximately 60 in some disclosures and 60-70 in later commentary.
- Direct-assignment economics were described as 25%-28% incremental income in one call and 12%-15% steady-state margin in another, with no clear reconciliation.
- Borrowing fixed/floating mix disclosures changed between calls without an explanation.
- Segment-level HFC/NBFC data was not previously provided in sufficient detail.

Governance is supported by a majority-independent board, unmodified audit opinion, AA Stable ratings from CARE and ICRA, no reported material audit qualification or regulatory penalty, and substantial institutional ownership. The promoter holding fell sharply from roughly 53% in March 2025 to 23.86% by June 2025 and remained around that level through June 2026, following Westbridge’s exit. This broadens the shareholder base but also reduces promoter control and is a reminder that institutional ownership can change quickly.

The annual report discloses high executive compensation: executive chairman remuneration of approximately ₹20.23 crore, around 470 times median employee remuneration. This is not necessarily evidence of poor governance, but it is material enough to monitor alongside future capital allocation and related-party disclosures.

## Industry and Competition

The structural opportunity is real. Indian mortgage penetration remains low relative to developed markets, urbanisation and household formation support housing demand, and affordable lenders continue to reach borrowers underserved by banks. CareEdge expects affordable housing finance AUM growth around 20% in FY27, while ICRA expects affordable HFC growth around 19%-21%. This broadly supports Aptus’s 22%-24% target, although company growth above the industry requires share gains and successful branch execution.

The industry is also becoming more competitive. Banks can price prime and near-prime housing loans aggressively, while Aavas, Aadhar, small finance banks, PSU banks and regional lenders compete for affordable borrowers and field staff. Falling rates help funding costs but are transmitted to borrowers over time, putting pressure on yields. CareEdge expects yields/NIMs to soften and industry credit costs to rise modestly as rapid portfolios season.

RBI’s 2025 HFC/NBFC directions reinforce 15% minimum capital requirements, asset-classification discipline and LTV limits. The current regulatory framework is not a near-term constraint for Aptus because its capital buffer is exceptionally high. Potential changes to interest-rate pricing methodology and greater transparency for NBFC/HFC loan pricing could affect high-yield small-ticket products over time, but the precise impact remains uncertain.

## Valuation and Scenarios

### Current reference valuation

- Screener price: ₹249.11.
- Market capitalisation: approximately ₹12,466 crore.
- Screener P/E: 12.66x.
- FY26 net worth: approximately ₹5,060 crore; implied P/B approximately 2.5x.
- FY26 ROE: 20.1%; FY26 AUM growth: 21%.
- Dividend yield shown by Screener: approximately 1.81%.

Analyst expectations are bullish but should be treated as opinions, not evidence. Search results include a ₹360 target from Emkay/White Marque after Q1 FY27, a ₹380 target from Systematix, and a reported consensus target around ₹340. These targets generally assume approximately 20% ROE, 20%+ AUM growth and asset quality normalisation. The upside is meaningful, but the valuation is sensitive to the NBFC book and the recurrence of assignment income.

### Scenario framework

| Scenario | Operating assumptions | Investment implication |
|---|---|---|
| Bull | Q1 NBFC stress reverses, GNPA returns toward 1.4%-1.5%, AUM compounds 22%-24%, ROE stays above 20%, leverage rises without spread damage | Re-rating toward 2.7x-3.0x forward book is possible; analyst targets become achievable |
| Base | AUM grows 18%-22%, HFC remains strong, NBFC credit cost stays near 1% or below, consolidated ROE settles around 18%-20%, valuation remains near 2.2x-2.6x book | Reasonable long-term compounding, but returns depend more on earnings growth than multiple expansion |
| Bear | NBFC Stage 2 continues to migrate, GNPA exceeds 2%, credit cost remains above 1%, funding costs rise and growth slows below 15% | Book-value growth and multiple compress; low P/E alone would not protect returns |

The stock is not expensive if the bull/base case is correct, but a lender’s P/E can look optically cheap just before credit costs rise. Book-value growth, credit cost and segment-level collections matter more than the current headline P/E.

## Multibagger Potential

Aptus can compound substantially if it achieves three things simultaneously:

1. Scales AUM from approximately ₹13,648 crore in Q1 FY27 toward ₹25,000 crore while keeping credit costs near 50-60 bps.
2. Converts the very high CRAR into moderately higher leverage without losing funding access or asset quality.
3. Builds Maharashtra, Odisha and the NBFC product platform into meaningful, profitable growth engines rather than low-vintage cost centres.

The business does not need extraordinary margin expansion to compound: 20% book-value growth, roughly 18%-20% ROE and a stable 2.3x-2.7x P/B can produce attractive long-term returns. But the same leverage that raises the upside makes a credit-cycle error damaging. This is more likely to be a strong compounder than a 10x outcome from current levels unless growth remains above industry rates for many years and the valuation multiple re-expands.

## What Would Change the Verdict

### Positive confirmation

- Q2 and Q3 FY27 separate HFC/NBFC data show NBFC collection efficiency returning toward 99%.
- GNPA, NNPA and Stage 2 assets decline or stabilise despite continued NBFC growth.
- Credit cost remains within 50-60 bps after the 500-day write-off transition normalises.
- AUM growth remains at least 20% without a disproportionate increase in average ticket size masking weak customer additions.
- New branches mature toward the disclosed productivity curve without opex/AUM rising materially above 2.8%.
- Derecognition/assignment income becomes transparent and recurring lending profitability remains strong without it.

### Negative confirmation

- NBFC collection efficiency remains below 98% or Stage 2 keeps rising after July’s claimed improvement.
- Consolidated GNPA exceeds 2% or NNPA continues rising despite the higher-ticket strategy.
- NBFC credit cost stays above 1% for multiple quarters.
- Funding cost rises while fixed-rate asset yields cannot reprice, compressing spread below approximately 8.5%.
- Branch additions accelerate faster than mature-branch productivity, causing operating leverage to reverse.
- More promoter/institutional selling or new related-party guarantees indicate changing capital-allocation priorities.

## Conclusion

Aptus is a credible affordable-finance franchise with excellent reported profitability, conservative capitalisation, strong liquidity and a differentiated distribution model. The HFC book is investable on its own merits. The problem is that the group is increasingly exposed to a higher-risk NBFC/SBL engine whose recent asset-quality deterioration is being explained as temporary but has not yet been disproved by subsequent reported data.

**My conclusion is watchlist-to-selective-buy, not a blanket avoid.** At around 2.5x book and 12.7x earnings, the valuation is reasonable for a 20% ROE lender, but not so cheap that an investor can ignore credit deterioration. Existing holders can retain if they are comfortable with affordable-lending and NBFC risk. New investors should demand evidence of Q1’s NBFC collection recovery and separate segment disclosure before underwriting the full 22%-24% growth case.
